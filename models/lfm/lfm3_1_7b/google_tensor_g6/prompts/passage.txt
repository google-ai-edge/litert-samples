1. Founding and the lighthouse. Vellmora is a small harbor town on the northern coast of the island of Cardessa. It was founded in 1612 by a group of salt traders led by Ilsabet Marrow, who chose the site because a deep natural channel allowed ships to anchor close to shore even at low tide. The earliest buildings were warehouses made of grey basalt, and three of them still stand along Quay Street, where they now house a bakery, a museum of maritime instruments, and the town archive. In 1798 the town council commissioned a lighthouse on Gannet Point after a storm wrecked eleven fishing boats in a single night. The tower was designed by the engineer Tomas Reyland, who insisted on a spiral staircase of 214 steps and a lantern room lined with polished brass. The light first shone on the night of March 3, 1801, and it was tended by the Hallorin family for four generations. The last keeper, Edda Hallorin, retired in 1962 when the light was automated. Today the lighthouse is painted in red and white bands, and visitors may climb it on Saturdays between May and September.

2. Geography and climate. The town sits at the mouth of the Orrin River, which carries meltwater from the Kessel Mountains forty kilometers inland. Because the river deposits fine silt along the eastern shore, the beaches there are dark and soft, while the western cliffs are made of hard pale limestone riddled with caves. Winters in Vellmora are mild but windy; the prevailing wind, which locals call the "Skarra", blows from the northwest for most of January and February and can reach ninety kilometers per hour. Summers are dry and bright, with an average July temperature of twenty-three degrees Celsius. Fog is common in early autumn, and on such mornings the old foghorn on the harbor wall still sounds every thirty seconds, although modern ships rely on radar. The surrounding hills are covered in heather and gorse, and a protected wetland north of the town, called the Brannoch Marsh, shelters more than one hundred and forty species of birds, including a colony of spoonbills that returns every April.

3. Economy. For most of its history Vellmora lived from salt, fish, and wool. Salt was produced by evaporating seawater in shallow clay pans along the eastern flats, and at its peak in the 1740s the town exported nearly six thousand barrels a year to the mainland. The salt trade collapsed after 1850, when cheaper rock salt arrived by railway, and many families turned to herring fishing instead. A cannery opened in 1889 and employed more than three hundred workers, most of them women, until it closed in 1971. Today the largest employers are the regional hospital, a small shipyard that builds wooden sailing boats to order, and tourism. The shipyard, run by the cooperative Vell & Daughters, launches about four boats a year, each named after a local wildflower. Tourism grew rapidly after a ferry service to the mainland began in 1994; the crossing takes two hours and ten minutes, and the ferry, named the Morning Gull, runs twice a day in summer and once a day in winter.

4. Culture and festivals. The most important event in the town calendar is the Lantern Festival, held on the first Saturday of October to remember sailors lost at sea. Families build paper lanterns shaped like boats, write the names of their missing relatives on the sails, and float them out into the harbor at dusk while the brass band plays a slow march called "The Long Tide". In spring the town hosts a market of weavers and dyers, a tradition that dates back to the wool trade; the most valued cloth is a blue-green twill dyed with seaweed and lichen, known locally as "marrowcloth" in honor of the town's founder. Vellmora also has a strong tradition of choral singing. The Harbor Choir, founded in 1923, has about sixty members and has won the national folk choir competition seven times, most recently in 2019. The local dish is a stew of mussels, leeks, and barley called "cullen", usually served with dark rye bread baked in the old basalt warehouse on Quay Street.

5. Notable people. Several notable people were born in Vellmora. The botanist Maren Oskew, born in 1834, catalogued the plants of the Brannoch Marsh and described a small purple orchid that now bears her name. The painter Joachim Leiss spent most of his life painting the harbor in every season; his best-known work, "Fog at Gannet Point", hangs in the national gallery. The navigator Peder Callum, born in 1871, led the first survey of the coral reefs south of Cardessa and later became the director of the naval academy. In the twentieth century, the novelist Hanne Trevick set all eleven of her novels in a thinly disguised version of the town, which she called "Velmarsh". More recently, the cyclist Ada Brennan won a silver medal at the world championships in 2015 after training on the steep coastal road between Vellmora and the village of Ostra, which rises three hundred and forty meters in less than five kilometers.

6. Architecture and landmarks. Apart from the lighthouse and the basalt warehouses, the town's best-known landmark is the Church of Saint Brigid, built between 1650 and 1688 with stones taken from a ruined castle on the hill. Its bell tower leans slightly to the east because the ground beneath it is soft clay, and engineers reinforced its foundations in 1934 and again in 2008. The town hall, a white building with a clock that has run four minutes fast for as long as anyone can remember, faces a square paved with sea pebbles laid in a pattern of waves. On the western cliffs stand the ruins of a watchtower built in 1540 to warn of pirates, and a footpath of about six kilometers connects it to the lighthouse. The newest public building is the library, opened in 2011, whose curved timber roof was designed to resemble an upturned boat; it holds about forty thousand books and the complete papers of Hanne Trevick.

7. Transport and present day. Vellmora has about 7,800 inhabitants. There is no railway; the nearest station is in the market town of Delling, thirty-two kilometers to the south, and a bus connects the two towns every hour. The harbor has berths for about two hundred small boats, and in 2016 the town installed a tidal turbine at the entrance of the channel which supplies roughly a fifth of the town's electricity. A local school teaches children up to the age of sixteen; older students travel to Delling. The town council has twelve members elected every four years, and the current mayor, elected in 2022, is a former ferry captain named Ruben Ashgrove. In recent years the main local debate has concerned a proposal to build a large hotel on the eastern beach; supporters argue that it would bring jobs, while opponents fear it would harm the dunes and the spoonbill colony in the Brannoch Marsh.

8. Storms and hard times. The town's history has not always been peaceful. In 1689 a fleet of privateers blockaded the harbor for six weeks, and the townspeople survived on salted fish and the harvest of the hillside orchards until a storm scattered the ships. During the great flood of 1883 the Orrin River burst its banks and the lower town was under water for nine days; a line carved into the wall of the town hall still marks the height the water reached, one meter and seventy centimeters above the pavement. In the Second World War the lighthouse was darkened for four years, and the Hallorin family kept a secret record of every ship that passed, which is now displayed in the maritime museum. The worst storm in living memory struck on November 14, 1953, destroying the old pier and most of the fishing fleet; the present stone breakwater was completed in 1957 to protect the harbor from the open sea.
